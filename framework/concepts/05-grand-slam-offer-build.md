# Concept 05 · The Grand Slam Offer: definition and the five-step build

**Books:** Offers (Start Here, Ch.2, Ch.3, Ch.9, Ch.10). Money Models supplies the "money model" component (concepts 21–25). Leads supplies the demand side (concepts 11–20).

## Definitions
- **Offer** = the goods and services you agree to provide, **how you accept payment**, and **the terms of the agreement**. Payment method and terms are part of the offer. [stated, OF-001]
- Quality ladder [stated, OF-002]: no offer → no business · bad → negative profit · decent → no profit, stagnation · good → some profit · Grand Slam → "fantastic profit". No thresholds define the rungs. **Do not turn this into a score.**
- **Two root problems** [stated, OF-003]: not enough clients; not enough cash. Mechanism: acquiring clients costs money and time and comes out of margin; prospects compare with cheaper options, so owners cut price (race to the bottom).
- **Commodity** = available from many places, prone to being bought on price; the prospect concludes the options are basically alike and picks the cheaper one. **Differentiated** = value-driven, "category of one." The work can be identical in both; the offer changes how it is perceived and compared. [stated, OF-009]

## The five-step build (OF-034)
| Step | Name | Output |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Identify the dream outcome | One specific destination |
| 2 | List problems | Every obstacle the buyer expects, in the order they meet them |
| 3 | Solutions list | Each problem reversed into a solution, then named |
| 4 | Create solution delivery vehicles ("the how") | Many ways to deliver each solution |
| 5a | Trim | Remove low-value / high-cost items |
| 5b | Stack | Bundle the rest into one named, valued deliverable |
[FLAG] The author calls Ch.10 "the most important" section and also Step 4 "the most important step." Do not rank steps on this.
The enhancers (scarcity, urgency, bonuses, guarantees, naming; concepts 06–10) are applied **after** this core offer exists.

### Step 1 · Dream outcome (OF-035)
- Sell the **destination**, not the vehicle (example: the author says he sold the vacation, not the membership or the plane flight) [stated]
- Book's example: "Lose 20lbs in 6 weeks" = a big dream outcome with a decreased time delay. [stated] "Ideally with a time frame" is our inference.

### Step 2 · List problems (OF-036)
- Think about what happens **immediately before and immediately after** someone uses the product. Order the list **in the sequence the buyer will experience each obstacle**. "Insane detail." More problems is better. The buckets are a prompt, not a limit. [stated]
- Each "thing they must do" is checked against four negative thoughts, one per value driver [stated]:
  - Dream Outcome: "This will not be financially worth it"
  - Perceived Likelihood: the prospect thinks it won't work for them specifically, that they can't stick with it, and that outside factors will interfere (author: "the most unique and service-specific" bucket)
  - Effort & Sacrifice: the prospect thinks it will be too hard or confusing, that they won't enjoy it, and that they will be bad at it
  - Time Delay: the prospect thinks it will take too much time, that they are too busy, that results will come too slowly, and that it won't be convenient
- Counting claim [stated]: there are 16 core problems, each with two to four sub-problems under it, for 32 to 64 problems in all. [derived] 16×2 = 32, 16×4 = 64 ✓. The text does not show where 16 comes from; the example has 4 "things they must do" (buy, cook, eat, exercise) × 4 drivers = 16.

### Step 3 · Solutions list (OF-037)
- Two sub-steps: (1) turn each problem into a solution by asking what you would need to show a person so that the problem gets solved. (2) **name** the solutions. Technique: add "How to…" and reverse the problem.
- Rule: "You will solve every problem." The how comes in Step 4.
- Claim, unsupported: if even one need is missing "it can cause someone not to buy."

### Step 4 · Delivery vehicles and the Delivery Cube (OF-039, figure)
For **each** problem, list every way you could solve it ("anything you could possibly do," including things you would not do). Done once per product. The six dimensions [figure; text and figure match one-to-one]:
1. Personal attention: one-on-one · small group · one-to-many
2. DIY / DWY (do it with them) / DFY (done for them)
3. Support: in-person, phone, email, text, Zoom, chat (live delivery)
4. Consumption: audio, video, written (recorded); figure also shows "live"
5. Speed & convenience: reply times and hours (24/7, 9–5, within 5 min / 1 hour / 24 hours; figure adds "M–F?")
6. **10x & 1/10th test**: ask what you would deliver if customers paid 10x your price (or $100,000), and how you could still make them successful if they paid 1/10th.
Worked example for one problem ("Buying healthy food is hard…"): 6 one-on-one options, 4 small-group, 6 one-to-many.
Do not merge this cube with the Leads lead-magnet delivery dimensions (LD-008); they differ.

### "Solve every perceived problem" (OF-040)
Avoid being sentimental about exactly how you solve the problem. Not solving one does not mean you won't sell; you will sell to fewer people than you otherwise could. Goal: to sell to as many people as possible, at the highest achievable price and the highest achievable margin. The eating-out guide anecdote is a [claim].

### Step 5a · Trim (OF-041)
| Cost to you | Value to buyer | Action |
|---|---|---|
| high | low | remove first |
| low | low | remove second |
| low | high | keep |
| high | high | keep (reserve high-cost items "for big value adds only") |
- Judge **value** with the Value Equation: which items the person (a) financially values, (b) believes make success likely, (c) feels need much less effort and sacrifice, (d) will see results from with far less time. [stated]
- If a high-value item is too costly (e.g., living with the client): find a lesser version you can deliver at scale; step back one level at a time until time or cost is acceptable, **or raise the price**. [stated]
- Preference: **one-to-many** solutions have the biggest gap between cost and value (high one-time creation cost, "infinitely low" extra effort afterward). [stated]
- [FLAG] The Ch.10 recap says trim to "the highest value and lowest cost," narrower than the rule (which also keeps high/high). Use the rule as written.
- **Not in the book:** thresholds for "high" vs "low" cost or value.

### Step 5b · Stack (OF-042)
- Format: Problem → solution wording → a "sexier name" for the bundle → delivery vehicles → stated dollar value. [stated]
- Example [stated]: seven bundles: grocery $1,000 · cooking $600 · meal plan $500 · workouts $699 · travel blueprint $199 · accountability $1,000 · eating-out $349. Book total: "$4,351 (!) All for only $599."
- [derived] The seven sum to **$4,347**, not $4,351 (off by $4). Value ÷ price = 7.26x.
- Stated functions: solves **all** perceived problems; conviction the offer is one of a kind; impossible to compare with the business down the street.
- [our note] The bundle values are the author's own judgments; no method is given. A tool should treat them as user inputs flagged as estimates.

## Sequencing rule (OF-038, figure)
- Figure: two arrows between **hard to sell / easy to fulfill** and **easy to sell / hard to fulfill**. Goal: a sweet spot, easy to sell and easy to fulfill. [stated]
- Mantra [stated]: **"Create flow. Monetize flow. Then add friction."** Generate demand first; only then add friction in marketing or offer less for the same price. Reason: without demand you cannot tell whether your offering is any good.
- First Grand Slam Offer: **over-deliver "like crazy"**; fund fixes from cash flow later (OF-043). Target: "$100,000 things for only $10,000." [stated]
- [claim] Gym Launch case: 21 days flown out, owner paid a $500 refundable reservation, ~$100,000/mo in **up-front cash** sold (cash collected, not income or profit), team of 8; later taught owners at about a third of the price. Promise unchanged; how and what changed.

## Convergent vs divergent thinking and the sales-capacity formula (OF-033)
- Convergent example [stated]: 3 salespeople, each can take **100 calls/month**; **4 calls = 1 sale (including no-shows)**; goal **110 sales** → hire **2 more** (5 total).
- Formula [derived from the example; the book gives only the example]: closes per rep per month = calls per rep ÷ calls per sale = 100 ÷ 4 = **25**; reps needed = **ceiling**(sales goal ÷ closes per rep) = ceiling(110 ÷ 25 = 4.4) = **5**; reps to hire = 5 − 3 = **2**.
- [our note] "Calls per sale" here counts all calls including no-shows, which differs from the show-rate and closing-rate steps in the funnel chain below. A tool must say which definition it uses.
- Divergent thinking [stated]: many right answers, one "way more right." The **brick exercise** (list uses of a brick in 120 seconds; vary size, material, shape) trains seeing many ways to create value from one asset; every offer has building blocks that can be combined many ways (this feeds Steps 4–5).

## Funnel money math (OF-011, figures): how to compute, not what to expect
Chain: Ad spend → Impressions → Response rate → Appts booked → Show rate → Appts showed → Closing % → Appts closed → Price → Cash up front → ROAS. [figure]
Relationships [derived; the figure shows values, not formulas]:
- Booked ≈ Impressions × Response rate · Showed = Booked × Show rate · Closed = Showed × Closing % · Cash = Closed × Price · ROAS = Cash ÷ Ad spend.
Example [figure]: Commodity vs Grand Slam. Spend $10,000 both; 300,000 impressions; response 0.00013 vs 0.00033; booked 40 vs 100; show 75%; showed 30 vs 75; close 16% vs 37%; closed 5 vs 28; price $1,000 vs $3,997; cash $5,000 vs $112,000; ROAS 0.5:1 vs 11.2:1. Author's differences: 2.5x response, 2.3x closing, 4x price, **22.4x** cash.
- [FLAG] Text says 2.5 × 2.5 × 4 = 22.4x; that equals **25**. With the figure's 2.3x closing it is 23.0. 22.4x is right only from the **counts** (5.6 × 3.997). **Compute from counts and rates, never from the text's shorthand.**
- [FLAG] The $3,997 price appears only in the figure. Show-rate explanation in the figure is mislabeled (copy of the response-rate wording). Response rate is a fraction labeled "percentage." Closing % uses rounded values (5/30 = 16.7%).
- [FLAG] The commodity payback narrative (5 customers pay another $1,000 in 30 days → $10,000 = break-even) uses **cash**, not gross profit, and ignores delivery cost. Not usable as a gross-profit payback without adding COGS.
- Values are "rounded for illustration," from a lead-generation agency. [claim] Do not use as benchmarks.
- [FLAG] OF-011: the text also calls this the very Grand Slam Offer the author's own software business, which serves agencies, used while attributing the numbers to a lead-generation agency; the book does not reconcile these. The Grand Slam guarantee in the example is that 20 people arrive in the first month, or the next month is free (the text says "people", not appointments).
