# Concept 06 · Scarcity (and the supply/demand model behind the enhancers)

**Books:** Offers (Ch.11, Ch.12). Leads (one step in the lead-magnet CTA, a script slot). Money Models (giveaway limits).
**Built from entries:** OF-044, OF-045, OF-046, OF-047, OF-048, OF-049, OF-050, OF-051, OF-052, OF-053 · LD-012 · MM-016.
**Tag key:** [stated] · [figure] · [derived] · [FLAG] · [claim] · [our note].

## Definition
Scarcity = a **fixed supply or quantity** available for purchase ("fear of missing out"). You publicly share that you are giving away only X products or can take only Y new clients. [stated, OF-049]
- **Scarcity is a function of quantity; urgency is a function of time.** (OF-054; see concept 07.)
- Three types [stated]: (1) limited **seats/slots** (in general or over a period); (2) limited supply of **bonuses**; (3) **never available again**.
- Claim, unsourced: the fear of losing something is stronger than the wish to gain something. [stated]

## Why it works, as the author argues
- **Implied demand** (OF-048): authorities charge more because people assume large demand for their time, so supply is small.
- Value has two components that compound: how rare the source is, and the actual value delivered. [stated] (OF-048 says "compound"; it gives no multiplication or formula.)
- Whoever needs the trade less always has the advantage. [stated]
- Scarcity "implies within it, social proof." (OF-052)
- [claim] 60-minute project → $5M added profit; two offers of $50,000 for a day (declined).

## The five enhancers and their stated mechanisms (OF-044)
| Enhancer | Mechanism (text) |
|---|---|
| Scarcity | decreases supply to raise prices, and **indirectly** raises demand through perceived exclusiveness |
| Urgency | increases demand by **decreasing the action threshold** |
| Bonuses | increase demand (and perceived exclusivity) |
| Guarantees | increase demand by **reversing risk** |
| Names | re-stimulate demand and expand awareness |
Chapters: scarcity 12, urgency 13, bonuses 14, guarantees 15, naming 16. Scope note: the Author Note says only scarcity, urgency, bonuses are broken down, yet guarantees and naming follow. Treat all five as in scope. Other persuasion forces (commitment and consistency, status, peer pressure, goodwill, celebrity endorsement, competition) are **not** covered; the book defers them. [FLAG]
[FLAG] Text says scarcity works by decreasing **supply**; the figure draws all five as arrows shifting **demand** right. **Do not model scarcity as a pure supply cut.**

## Supply-and-demand model (OF-045, figures; qualitative, no numbers)
- Figure 1: units (vertical) vs. price (horizontal); downward demand line, upward supply line, crossing at "Theoretical Max Revenue Price"; a point at high price/few units is "Max Profit Per Unit."
- Figure 2: five arrows (bonuses, scarcity, urgency, guarantees, naming) shift old demand to new demand → a new max revenue price and max profit per unit.
- Stated: marketing as a whole aims to shift the supply and demand curve. The "perfect profit combination" is lots of demand and very little supply, or perceived supply. Desire comes from not having what you want, so to raise demand you must **decrease or delay** satisfying prospects (sell fewer units than you could). Assumption: an ordinary business that is not aiming for mass-market reach.
- [our note] The figures have no numbers; **a tool must not invent a demand curve.**

## The demand pyramid (OF-046)
[figure] Willing buyers by price: $5,000 ×2 · $2,000 ×3 · $500 ×5 (10 total). The $2,000 tier is in the figure only.
- Scenario one [stated]: 10 units at $500. Scenario two [stated]: two one-day 1-on-1 workshops at $5,000. [derived] Revenue: **$5,000** and **$10,000** (the figures are labeled $5000 and $10,000). [derived] Selling the middle tier would also be 5 × $2,000 = $10,000 (not in the text).
- Claimed benefits of scenario two: more revenue, lower costs, more value delivered, **pent-up demand** among the 8 non-buyers. Selling to the whole pyramid satisfies all demand: zero pent-up demand for next time.
- Follow-on: next time open **three** spots at the same price and sell them all. Selling at $500 again would likely sell fewer.
- Rule ("Hormozi Law"): **the longer you postpone the ask, the larger the request you are able to make.** Operating rule: keep supply under the demand you can generate. Caution: zero supply earns nothing; satisfying all demand "kills the golden goose."
- [claim] "Fractal (80/20)": one fifth of prospects are ready to pay 5x the price or even more. The example shows 2 of 10 paying 10x. Do not derive a multiple from the text.
- [our note] 10 prospects and 3 prices is an illustration, **not evidence** that skimming beats volume. A tool should compute revenue and margin by tier from the user's own data and show the comparison, not assert the conclusion.
- Fundraiser (OF-047) [claim]: ticket $15,000 → $25,000, 100 attendees, ~$5.4M raised ($54,000 per head; [FLAG] the author and his wife were themselves donating $1,000,000 that night, which is part of the $5.4M, so the per-head figure is not all outside buyers); a charity setting; not a pricing benchmark.

## How to apply (OF-050, 051, 052, 053)
**Physical products:** limited releases; **always sell out** (better than over-ordering); **announce** the sell-out; repeated drops compound ("once a month" is the sweet spot for companies the author knows [claim]).
**Services: three capacity caps** (OF-051)
| Cap | Rule | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Total business cap | Only X clients total at this service level | Waiting list; **periodically raise capacity 10–20%, then cap again**; best for highest tiers |
| Growth-rate cap | Only X new clients **per week** | e.g., a cap of 5 new clients each week, with 3 spots already claimed |
| Cohort cap | Only X clients **per class or cohort** | e.g., "100 clients 4 times a year" |
- Rule: always have **fewer spots than you think you can sell**. Best for higher-ticket upsells (workshops, trainings, events, consulting). Free lead-magnet example: only **20 new downloads per week** with a waiting list.
**Honest scarcity** (OF-052): define the number of clients you are **actually willing and able** to take, then advertise it ("three-fourths of the way to capacity this week," "81% to capacity"). The author says only you draw where "full" is, and calls it "arbitrary."
**Extreme scarcity** (OF-053): very limited 1-on-1 access, tiny cap, very high price; the author's caveat "(yes, unscalable)." **"Once you're out, you can never come back"**: cap a service level and bar returns; works best with small groups.

## Ethics guardrail
- [our note] The word "arbitrary" does not authorize a cap you do not honor. A tool must require the stated cap to be **real and enforced**.
- Leads (LD-012) gives a real-limit test: can you cope with 1000x as many customers arriving tomorrow? If not, you have a real limit; **advertise it** ("ethical scarcity"). Trade-off: the fewer you have, the fewer engaged leads you can get before running out.
- [FLAG] LD-012 also says "make up a reason" / "any reason (even bad ones)"; this conflicts with its own ethical-scarcity standard. Same pattern as the M-A-G-I-C "so long as you believe it" line (OF-076). Require true reasons.
- [FLAG] The Leads core-offer script includes a "looking for 5" line (LD-034); the case-study offer "five… that's all I can handle" (LD-020). Both are scarcity claims true only if the cap is real.

## Cross-book
- Money Models giveaways: limit by time (e.g., seven days), number of entries (e.g., 5,000), or both, matched to the people you have time and resources to connect with (MM-016).
- Leads: scarcity/urgency are the CTA reasons to act now (LD-012, LD-055, LD-091 launch "shout" cadence).

## Do not
- Do not invent numbers for a demand curve or price tiers.
- Do not state that people fear loss more than they desire gain or "80/20, 5x" as facts.
- Do not generate a cap the business will not enforce.
