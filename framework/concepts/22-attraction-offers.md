## What an attraction offer is
Attraction Offers bring in leads and turn them into paying customers; they convert ad spend into cash by giving something away or selling it cheaper. [stated, MM-007] The point: "turn strangers into customers" and do it so that more cash arrives up front; ideally the cash covers the cost of acquiring the customer and delivering the product several times over. (MM-027)
- **Free-to-discount continuum:** any "free" can also be "discount" or "$1" and vice versa; they all discount a product to some level. Figure: 100% off/FREE to 0% off/FULL PRICE; highest demand at free, lowest at full price (no numbers; do not make a demand curve). More and better free stuff: the customer might have to buy one item to get two free ones, and that is how free offers earn money. [stated]
- [FLAG] The contents list six items under Section II; the sixth ("Free Goodwill Offer") is a request for a book review and not an offer mechanic (MM-026). Five offers.
- [FLAG] The target "multiple times over" is stricter than "cover costs within 30 days" and has no number; see concept 01.
- [claim] Turning $1,000 into $10,000,000 took them ten months, because the author kept doubling down as returns came in. No breakdown; not revenue-or-profit defined.

## 1. Win Your Money Back (MM-009–013)
**Definition:** The customer is given a goal and the steps to reach it; anyone who gets there qualifies for a refund, or for that amount as store credit. Tagline: complete the required action in the set time, following the set rules, and the product is free. Figure: Do X / in Y time / within Z rules.
**Three qualification types:** **Results** (customer bets on own ability), **Actions** (follows directions), **Actions and Results** (both). The offer must make "the results and actions simple to track."
**Examples:** B2C "Free 28-Day Blueprint" (deposit; attend calls, post weekly, journal daily, attend feedback and transformation sessions); B2B "5 Customers In 5 Days Free Challenge" (100 messages/day, stats, daily training, day-5 call); physical: a car-mileage offer where reaching 1,000,000 miles earns a free car (credit toward your next car; "an actual offer" [claim]).
**Rules and economics:**
- Why it makes money [stated]: many won't qualify; those who qualify often stay but need something else to buy, so **have an upsell ready to apply their winnings**. Most of the money comes from customers who succeed, provided you have another offer ready for them.
- **Refund rules:** run it only when refunds stay under 5%; if they do not, repair the product. Offer it only when returning money does not bother you; about **10%** of customers will ask for their money back [claim, "thousands of gyms"]. [FLAG] 10% vs 5% unreconciled (which base?). No qualify rate or payout share is given, so net economics can't be computed.
- Three criteria that "make or break": easy to track · gets customers results ("Realistic criteria do just fine") · advertises the business (posts, reviews, referrals).
- Store credit vs cash [claim]: same number of customers; to keep calling it free in ads, add a no-conditions satisfaction guarantee (an option, not a requirement); "Check with legal counsel." [FLAG] "free" vs credit applied to a further purchase.
- Policy: when a customer requests a refund, whether or not they are entitled, the author grants it.
- Fits "stuff people start and quit": starting businesses, learning skills, fitness, beauty, time management. [claim] "over $1 billion in sales industry-wide."
**Applying the credit (MM-012):** apply it to something that **costs more** than their winnings, over a longer period. Service $200/month; $600 credit; not three free months but **$600 over 12 months = $50/month off → $150/month**. [derived ✓] "people fall off if they don't pay something" [claim].
**Meetings and "make everyone a winner" (MM-013):** every meeting is an offer point (nutrition orientation → supplements; progress check-in → membership; transformation feedback → membership, prepay discount). Then: around the midpoint, present your next offer as though they have already won and "let the 'losers' win" at the end: credit the whole deposit toward staying "whether you hit the short-term goal or not."
- [FLAG: ethics/transparency] The ad says money back **if you do X**; the tactic credits the deposit toward the next purchase regardless, and says do it "in private." That conflicts with Note 6 "Be Transparent" if the original terms said otherwise. Before client use: state the real terms in the ad (including that credit may be applied to a further purchase), check local ad law, decide whether the credit is genuinely conditional.
- [FLAG: economics] If everyone is a winner, the offer is "deposit credited toward the next purchase," and the profit comes entirely from the follow-on offer. Keep the three tactics separate: meetings as offer points (low risk), mid-program credit, end-of-program credit.
- Principle: the sale exists to win the customer; the customer is not just a way to get a sale.

## 2. Giveaways (MM-014–016)
**Definition:** advertise a chance to win a big prize in exchange for contact information (and what else you want); after picking a winner, offer everyone else the prize at a discounted price. Also "scholarship, sweepstakes, raffle." Boxed disclaimer: "heavily regulated… can become illegal lotteries"; "in no way a guarantee of lawfulness." Consult counsel.
**Six steps:** 1 Pick a Grand Prize (the thing you want everyone to buy; assign a monetary value as a price anchor) · 2 Pick the promotional offer (discount, bonus or minor change that "ethically justifies" a price reduction) · 3 Ask for contact info and permission to contact, plus eligibility questions · 4 Pick qualifying actions · 5 **Deadline of three to seven days**, daily updates, value along with the countdown · 6 Announce the winner publicly; message everyone else privately and ask them to book a call. Second deadline: the promotional offer expires in seven days. Whisper–Tease–Shout (Leads LD-091).
**Cost-to-value rule:** set the discount on your core offer at 10% to 30% of your gross margins. Example: Grand Prize "$5,000 value with a $2,000 retail price tag"; everyone else $1,800 (10% off retail); the 10% price cut creates a 64% gap in cost-to-value. [derived ✓] 1,800 ÷ 5,000 = 36%.
- [FLAG] The 64% depends on a "value" 2.5x the retail price; the book doesn't say what makes up the extra. "10%–30% of gross margins" is ambiguous (dollars of margin vs percent off price); the example is 10% off price. Ask which.
- [FLAG] The optional "reason" ("you found their answers/story so compelling") conflicts with Note 6 if untrue.
**Four examples (retail → promo, [derived] discount if dollars off):** dentist $6,000 braces → $2,000 gift card (33%); dog food $1,000 → $300 gift card for subscribers (30%); services $5,000 → $2,000 voucher (40%); consulting $12,000 turnaround → $6,000 partial scholarship (50%). [FLAG] Unclear whether promo is dollars off or amount paid.
**Rules:** legal no-brainers: somebody must win; clear rules; more than one person can win. Eligibility questions (why this program, why now…): more work to enter = fewer but more qualified. A giveaway that fails means the Grand Prize was not big enough. **Two prizes:** a referrer wins too if their referral wins ("twice the leads (or more)" [claim]); Skool post screenshot says "top 10" (not the same rule). Scarcity: limit by time (seven days), entries (5,000), or both, matched to the number of calls you can make in seven days. **Urgency in three places: to enter, to claim, to use** ("always have deadlines"). Downsell: lead with the promo; then the same percentage on another product; for recurring businesses apply the discount over the longest period they agree to, then bill normal rates.

## 3. Decoy Offer (MM-017–019)
**Definition:** A Decoy Offer is advertised as free or discounted; when leads want details, you also show a higher-value premium offer with extra features, benefits, bonuses and guarantees. This gives you time to move them up to the premium rather than lose them, and in both cases every lead can be closed.
**Stories [claim]:** $5 five-day tanning pass → credit toward a $19.99 unlimited first month; fitness: free (once a week) vs "Ultimate" **$399**: "about eight out of ten people" took the $399 option (one business, no sample size; not a benchmark).
**Build:** Build the decoy from the premium offer with fewer parts, older models or fewer customized versions, and with no guarantees. "Make the contrast huge." Advertise benefits, not features.
**Four ways to say the same discount** ($100/month for a year sold for $900): 25% off · $300 off · 3 months free · one year for $900 ($1,200). [derived ✓] "Worth testing."
**Discount vs free:** free gets more leads; discount gets fewer leads but a higher show-up rate; try a discount where no-shows are costly (doctors, lawyers, dentists). [claim]
**Sales conversation:** premium first if possible. If they ask for the decoy: ask whether they came for something free or for results that last; present both, then ask which of the two they think will reach their goal sooner. Assumed close; "Almost bored over how regularly people buy." Optional "surprise benefit" to decoy takers.
- [FLAG: ethics/pressure] The line telling them they will now have to answer with the premium offer is a leading-question close; assumed close sits uneasily next to Note 4 ("Hard Selling Is For Weak Products"). The premium must actually be better for the customer. [FLAG] Sequencing: side-by-side (p.53, p.57) vs premium first (p.55). [FLAG] Decoy product differs across stories (priced pass, free product, free-or-$21). [FLAG] None of the three examples states the premium price; the user supplies premium price and take rate. [FLAG] Premium examples include guarantees; their cost must be modeled.

## 4. Buy X Get Y Free (MM-020–022)
**Definition:** A customer who buys one thing receives other things free; the more free items, and the more they are worth, the stronger the effect. Discount Offers attract far less notice than free ones. A **reframe, not a discount**: total paid and total goods unchanged.
**Math:** three shirts at $10 = $30, or "one shirt for $30, get 2 free" (same price, more free stuff). With a discount: three for $20 ($6.67 each, 33% off) or one for $20 with two free. Boot Factory [claim]: one pair $200; the offer was one pair for $600 with two more pairs at no charge. [FLAG] the $200 is illustrative; the only price seen was $600 for the pair.
**18-month service example:** Good "Buy 12 Months Get 6 Months Free" $1,800 · Better "Buy 9, Get 9 Free" $1,800 · Best "Buy 6, Get 12 Free" $1,800. [derived] $100 per month of access in each; implied price per paid month $150/$200/$300.
**Rules:** **permanently raise prices** to accommodate the discount (be honest: really do raise prices, for at least a season). More free than paid works better ("Buy one get two"). Free things can differ from paid (socks vs shirts). Test "buy one get two" vs a 33% discount. **Manage the money:** if you collect a year's payments in a month, make sure you can deliver for the year; selling something you cannot supply breaks the law. Existing customers: "Buy Ten and Get Two Free" at current price, limit to **10% of customers**. One-time buyers: "make them buy big." [claim] Previously a year to make money back; "Buy 6 Months Get 6 Months Free" attracted far more customers and was paid up front.
- [FLAGS] The statement that a deal giving ten free for two bought beats one giving two free for ten bought matches no example; socks text ($10 shirt) vs figure ($20 shirt); the book's own examples (6/6, 2/2) break "more free than paid"; "permanently" vs "for a season"; "doubled" vs "tripled"; "Buy Ten Get Two" reverses "more free than paid"; no formula or reserve percentage for delivering the prepaid period. [our note] Model cash received, delivery cost per period, and refund exposure separately.
- Summary: for stuff that makes sense to buy more of or get longer access to; if customers usually stay three months, a Buy 2 Get 2 Free deal will keep them for four months.

## 5. Pay Less Now or Pay More Later (MM-023–025)
**Definition:** The customer chooses between paying full price later or a discounted price now. All risk is taken off the customer, since they pay later and only when satisfied, so the offer combines delayed payment with a satisfaction guarantee.
**Mechanism:** the pay-later option lets you advertise "free" since they can choose not to pay; **card on file**; they can cancel any time before the charge. After they accept pay later, make a follow-up offer to pay now: **20–50% discount** and greater bonuses; pay-now gives up the guarantee. Story [claim]: reading course: $0 now/$297 tomorrow if the reading speed didn't double (must attend to be eligible) or $97 now + recordings. [derived] 67% off, outside 20–50%.
**Examples:** real-estate workshop $0 / billed $500 vs $299 pay-now (40% off) + upsell $30,000; hedges $0 / $599 vs $369 (38%) + $199/month lawncare; clothing trial $0 / $149 in 14 days vs $97 (35%) + subscription; return in like-new condition to qualify. [derived ✓]
**Rules:** a clear yes/no result (e.g., pain 1–10 before and after); **conditional guarantee** (customers may cancel the billing only if they meet the conditions; attendance, showing up, turning in data). Balance: too many take pay later → discount pay now more/add bonuses; too many take pay now → the opposite. **Cancellation threshold: when more than 10% of Pay Later customers cancel their payment, one of three things is wrong: too much was promised, the qualifying bar for the guarantee is set too leniently, or the price is too high.** Recurring version: pay a higher rate in 30 days or pay less today and keep the lower rate for good. "Hint at your next offer early."
- [FLAGS] Sequencing (both options at once vs follow-up); "remove all risk" vs conditional; the 10% base is people who chose pay later and "conditions are too low" is ambiguous; "everyone wants to know…" statements may be unsubstantiable (Note 6). **[FLAG: payments]** Charging a card after a free period is a payments-risk area: the book gives no consent, dispute or chargeback guidance beyond "Obey the law."

## Do not
- Do not advertise "free" without the real terms (credit, card on file, conditions).
- Do not use 80%, 10%, 5%, 64% or 20–50% as universal numbers; each is a stated pattern.
- Do not call Free Goodwill a sixth attraction offer.
