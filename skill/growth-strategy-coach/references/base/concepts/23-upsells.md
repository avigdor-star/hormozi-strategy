# Concept 23 · Upsell offers (Classic, Menu, Anchor, Rollover)

**Built from entries:** MM-028–MM-041 (and MM-012, MM-013 for the credit mechanics).
**Tag key:** [stated] · [figure] · [derived] · [FLAG] · [claim] · [our note].

## What an upsell is
"Upsells just mean whatever we offer next." Goal of Section III: "maximize 30-day profits." Solving one problem brings another into view, and you sell the fix for the new problem your offer exposes. Upsells frequently produce most of the profit and can decide whether a Money Model works. [stated, MM-028]
- Three kinds [stated]: **more** of what they got (quantity), **better** versions (quality), **new or complementary** stuff.
- Why upsells fail [stated]: you offer something they don't want; at the wrong time (before they've experienced the problem); the wrong way ("they don't believe you"); or a combination.
- Section warning [stated]: These tactics are very powerful and have to be used ethically.
- Fast definitions (MM-068 recap): **Classic:** offer the fix for the customer's next problem as soon as they become aware of it; the pitch is that X needs Y. **Menu:** tell customers which options they can skip, then state what they do need: skip that one, take this one. **Anchor:** offer your priciest item first; if the customer hesitates, offer a far cheaper option that still works for them. **Rollover:** apply part or all of what the customer already paid as credit toward your next offer.

### The burger example (MM-028) — use the figure's numbers
- Figure "BURGER, FRIES & SODA METHOD": old way: price $2.00, cost $1.75, profit $.25. Upsell 1 (medium): price $2.00, cost $.25, profit $1.75 → **8x** gross profit/sale. Upsell 2 (large): price $1.00, cost $.10, profit $.90 → **11.6x**. [derived ✓] $2.00 ÷ $.25 = 8; $2.90 ÷ $.25 = 11.6. Implied margins: burger 12.5%; upsell 1 87.5%; upsell 2 90%.
- [FLAG] The **text** does not tie: $0.25 + $0.75 + $1.75 = $2.75 (11x, not 8x); the third step says "$3.00" and "11.6x" (3.00 ÷ 0.25 = 12x). **Use the figure.** "~10,000 burgers a day" has no fixed-cost basis.
- Lesson: your best seller is not necessarily your biggest profit maker. [our note] A business with 12.5% margin on its first product becomes viable only by selling high-margin add-ons at the moment of need.

## 1. Classic Upsell: "You can't have X without Y" (MM-029, 030, 031)
**Structure:** your core offer solves one problem and creates another; your upsell immediately solves the next problem. It depends on understanding your customer's problem better than the customer does; when a problem appears and you can fix it right away for payment, do so. Why first: it is very profitable and simple, anyone can do it, and existing customers are always more likely to buy.
**Examples:** rental car (insurance, gas, late checkout); car wash → sealant; bicycle → helmet, lights, tires; exercise course → nutrition course. Fur-coat story [claim]: free earmuffs with coat storage; the seller then offers to store the earmuffs too for $30 and asks, expecting a no, whether anything else needs storing (earmuff cost, storage price, take rate not given).
**Rules [stated]:** "Actually Do It" (without this, what you have is a front end rather than a real business). **Offer the higher-profit option first.** "Say No To Say Yes." "Surprise and Delight" (with four bonuses saved for fence-sitters, add one at a time; if they say yes before, give all four, so "no one feels left out"). "Hyper Buying Cycle" (weddings, hobbies, babies, moving). "Use Free Bonuses To Create Problems Upsells Solve." Quicker access raises the value people place on it, and giving it to them before they say yes adds extra. **"If you bundle upsells, name them"** ("Fastest results," "Transformation package," "Minimum package"); you can "peel" items out as a downsell. Integrate upsells into other offers. **BAMFAM** (book-a-meeting-from-a-meeting). Keep upselling as often as it is sensible.
- [FLAG: pressure] "Say No To Say Yes" and giving it to them before they agree are pressure tactics next to Note 4; use only where the upsell serves the customer's problem.
**Magnetic middle (MM-031, figure):** three sizes; move the middle toward the one you want to push. Version A (more large buyers): Small $5, Medium $8 ("rather than the rational price of $7"), Large $9. Version B (more medium buyers): **text** Small $6 / Medium $7 / Large $9; **figure** Small $5 / Medium **$6** / Large $9. [FLAG] Text and figure disagree on Version B; the common rule is the figure's captions: "BRING MEDIUM CLOSER TO LARGE" / "…CLOSER TO SMALL." Bottom line: many buying small → bump to medium; many buying medium → bump to large; many buying large → "raise all your prices." Prices are illustrations, no take rates.
**Charge for guarantees (MM-031):** raise the price by 5% to 50% and offer, in return, a guarantee that the product delivers what you claim. [claim] Art studio: asked for an extra 10%; 30% of customers buy. [FLAG] "Pure profit" ignores replacement costs.

## 2. Menu Upsell (MM-032, 033, 034)
**Definition:** Steer customers away from options they can skip, then explain what they do need, what suits their preferences, and how to get its value from it. Four tactics, in order: **Unselling** → **Prescription upselling** → **A/B upselling** → **Card on file**. Figure: four panels.
- **Unselling:** explain what they don't need to build excitement for what they do ("cross that out").
- **Prescription:** when you have only one solution: explain how it integrates with what they bought and personalize/detail how to maximize it, "as if they already have."
- **A/B:** Ask whether they prefer A or B, so either answer is an upsell; offering a way to say no leads some people to say no. Make anything A/B-sellable (quantity, start dates, flavors, time slots, delivery speed, personnel, call or text). Add a "nudge" ("This is my favorite").
- **Card on file:** Asking whether to charge the card you already have saved lowers the hidden costs of buying. Pro Tip: for first purchases ask "What card do you wanna use?"
**Stories [claim]:** after 19 consults with no sale, the 20th: chocolate or vanilla? → "next twenty customers in a row"; step-by-step instructions on the order form ("prescription upselling"); out of product → crossed out what a customer didn't need ("unselling") and she "bought without hesitation."
**Examples:** massage therapist; dog food (beef or chicken?; joint chew each meal; heartworm wafer every 90 days); digital product ("You don't need all eight courses yet").
**Other rules:** When stock is gone, still take the payment and deliver later. "Employees love unselling." "Unselling lower-margin stuff where appropriate incentivizes higher-margin upsells."
- [FLAG: ethics] "we remove the option of not buying" conflicts with Note 4; "I kept products just to cross them out" risks theater; unselling is honest only where the customer truly doesn't need the item.
- [FLAG: payments] Card on file assumes the customer authorized the stored card for this purchase; charging for a new purchase without fresh authorization is a payments-risk point.
- [FLAG] Nudges like "a lot of people love this" are true only if true. Delay-delivery: the promised date must be met; no refund/delay-limit guidance.
**"Economist" play (MM-034, figure):** if you want customers to buy both: three options A, B, C (Both) with C priced the same as B. [claim] *The Economist*: Digital $59 / Digital+Print $125 → print plummeted; add Print-only $125 → customers took Digital+Print $125. Figure uses $10/$20. [FLAG] text vs figure numbers; unverified history. Differs from the Section II Decoy (a cheap lead-getter).

## 3. Anchor Upsell (MM-035, 036)
**Definition:** Offer the premium option first, and if the customer is shocked by the price, follow with a cheaper alternative that still works. Present a premium version **5x–10x** the price first; the main offer then looks like a better deal. They work best when the cheaper offer does the same main jobs as the premium version.
**Five steps:** (1) Present the Anchor. (2) Get "The Gasp." (3) Come to the rescue: ask if they care about what makes it premium. (4) Present your main offer (expect relief). (5) Ask how they want to pay ("Which card do you prefer?").
**Story [claim]:** budget $500; suit tags $16,000 then $2,200 + $300 accessories = $2,500 = 5.0x the budget. [derived] $16,000 ÷ $2,200 = 7.3x.
**Examples:** lawn care $1,000/week premium vs $200/week; painting $1,000 vs $200; newsletter $199/mo vs $19/mo ([derived] 5x, 5x, 10.5x). [FLAG] "bi-weekly yard maintenance" ambiguous.
**Rules:** treating the anchor as fake leads the customer to see it the same way: it must be real and deliverable; the premium must be something you truly want people to purchase. Only tweak secondary features; keep primary features the same. Pro Tip: making a $100 offer to someone with a $1,000 budget loses $900 vs losing $100 the other way. [claim] leading with the premium will not cost you customers (no numbers). [FLAG] ratios 5–10x (text), 10x (figure), 7.3x (suit) are not reconciled; "a fifth" vs "1/8th price." [FLAG: pressure] "The bigger the gasp, the more they bought" is a claim; the honesty condition is a genuine offer.

## 4. Rollover Upsell (MM-037–040)
**Definition:** Give part or all of what the customer paid before as credit toward the next offer. Three decisions: **who** (re-engage lapsed customers; rescue upset customers instead of refunding; "rescue" others' upset customers; upsell regular customers), **what** (more, better, new; "Roll their credit over to something more expensive"), **how** (up front or spread over time).
**Story [claim]:** Win Your Money Back winners put $600 toward three months of membership, then churned before their first out-of-pocket payment. The fix: **$50 off per month for 12 months** ($600 ÷ 12 ✓): they still get the money back, only spread over a year. [FLAG] The terms of won credit must be stated honestly (concept 22).
**Examples:** chiropractor (old patients, 6+ months: $500 credit to a new plan, applied up front); dentist (upset customer: $200 credit toward a whitening package); software (competitors' upset customers: credit remaining payments toward a longer agreement); membership ($600 first purchase → $50/month for 12 months).
**Pricing rule:** price the upsell at no less than four times the rollover credit; then even crediting the full amount gives a discount of at most 25%. You don't need to credit the entire amount; test the sweet spot. [our note] In MM-012 the $600 credit against a $2,400 12-month plan is exactly 25%.
**Other rules:** "Do Rollover Upsells before refunding" ("roll over for a 'do over'"). Previous customers (6+ months) are still customers. [claim] Personalized videos to 200 past customers, $4,000 credit each, about 20% took it, "an extra ~$1,900,000 of annual revenue" ([derived] 40 customers; $47,500 per returner; not stated what they bought). **One-time only:** Make it a single chance per customer, who can still buy at full price later, and check you still profit once the credit is applied.
- [FLAG: privacy/legal/ethics] "scraping contact information from negative product reviews" and opening a channel for people to air complaints about products across your industry are called "Nasty" by the author; scraping may break platform terms or privacy law; soliciting complaints raises unfair-competition issues. **Do not build a tool for this without a legal check.**
- [FLAG] "Do rollover before refunding" vs Note 3 ("give it back"): our reading is offer rollover first; if they still ask, refund. [FLAG] "one-time only" is honest only if the offer isn't repeated.
**"Famous" Gift Card Play (MM-040, figure):** advertise gift cards at 90%+ off (e.g., $200 card for $20), limit two per customer, only usable on other people; ask who it's for and for an introduction; make the card's value 20% of the price of what you sell next ("at least a $1,000 price tag"). [derived] 20% ✓; seller revenue on a $1,000 offer = $820. "People pay you to refer their friends." [FLAG: legal] Gift-card, consumer-protection and unclaimed-property rules vary; "pocket change from unused gift cards"; check counsel.

## Conclusion (MM-041)
"Solve rich people problems, they pay better." Four upsells; many businesses go overnight from losing cash to making it [claim]. Another wording of the break-even test: when your Attraction Offer already covers the cost of acquiring customers and delivering, extra money is welcome (concept 01).
