# Concept 03 · Pricing and price-to-value

**Books:** Offers (Ch.5, Ch.6 opening). Leads applies it in warm outreach (price steps). Money Models covers price *structure* (payment plans, discounts), see concepts 21–25.
**Built from entries:** OF-020, OF-021, OF-022, OF-023, OF-024, OF-032 (also OF-009).
**Tag key:** [stated] · [figure] · [derived] · [FLAG] · [claim] · [our note].

## Core rule
People buy when they believe **value received > price paid**; when value falls below price they stop buying. [stated, OF-020]
- The price/value relation is **qualitative**. The figure shows a falling "Value" line crossing a flat "Price" line at the "Point of Cancellation," with **no numeric axes**. [figure]
- Method: raise price **only after** increasing value, so the buyer still gets "money at a discount" (example: $100,000 of value for $10,000). [stated]
- Never charge more than the product is worth, but charge "far more than it costs to fulfill it," "up to a hundred times more." [stated, OF-032]

## Why not to price against competitors (OF-021)
Typical owner process [stated]: look at the market → see what others offer → take the average → go slightly below → give a little more → end with "more for less."
Result claimed [stated]: the market drifts to "perfect efficiency," where margins only cover survival. The "bottom 10–20% wash out" figure is an unsourced [claim].
Author's position: being second cheapest brings no strategic benefit, while being the most expensive does (Dan Kennedy quote); the goal is "the most money," not "the most customers."
Lowering price is capped at $0; raising it is not. Do not compete on price unless you have a breakthrough way to cut your costs to 1/10th. [stated; this sentence is in OF-020, not OF-021]

## Virtuous vs. vicious cycle of price (OF-022, figure)
| Effect on | Item | Price ↓ | Price ↑ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clients | Emotional investment | decrease | increase |
| Clients | Perceived value | decrease | increase |
| Clients | Results | decrease | increase |
| Clients | Demandingness | **increase** | decrease |
| Clients | Revenue for fulfillment per customer | decrease | increase |
| Business | Profit | decrease | increase |
| Business | Perceived value of self | decrease | increase |
| Business | Perception of impact (results) | decrease | increase |
| Business | Service levels | decrease | increase |
| Business | Sales team conviction | decrease | increase |

[stated, text only]: lower price attracts "the worst clients"; higher price attracts clients easiest to satisfy who "actually cost less to fulfill"; higher price funds systems, people and experience.
[FLAG] Text and figure overlap but are not identical (see OF-022). [our note] Every arrow is the author's argument, not tested evidence. Treat as a hypothesis, not a law.

## Price as a signal (OF-023)
- Higher price raises perceived value and "allure." The goal is to be priced so far above the market that buyers conclude something entirely different must be going on. [stated]
- A price that "stings a little" focuses attention and adherence for customers who must act to get the result. [stated]
- Condition [stated]: the product must deliver; conviction comes from experience.
- [claim] Wine study (same wine at three price labels; higher price rated better): unnamed, no citation. Do not cite as evidence.

## Cost-to-price gap (OF-032)
- Method stated: work out what people value most, triple down on it, and ruthlessly cut everything else.
- [claim] Photography case: average ticket **$300 → $1,500 (5x)** over two years; profit **$1,000/wk → $38,000/wk (38x)**. [derived] ratios check. The author has equity in this company. The 38x vs 5x gap means cost or volume also changed; the book gives no breakdown. **Do not use 5x → 38x as an expected result.**

## Gym Launch premium pricing case (OF-024, all [claim])
- Competitors charged $500/mo (low) and $5,000 (one high). Author priced a 16-week done-with-you intensive at **$16,000**. 35% of buyers were upsold into a 3-year, $42,000/yr agreement. Average gym-owner take-home cited as $35,280/yr.
- Survey (voluntary, 158 gyms, after 11 months): top-line +$19,932/mo; recurring +$13,339/mo; profit $2,943 → $8,940/mo; +67 clients; churn 10.7% → 6.8%/mo; price $129 → $167/mo.
- [FLAG] "32 times" compares a one-time $16,000 with a $500 **per month** price (not like-for-like). $8,940 ÷ $2,943 = 3.04x; the text says 3.1x.
- [our note] Self-reported by respondents only; says nothing about non-respondents. Not an expected outcome for clients.
- Nearly every business, 99 percent of them, needs higher prices in order to grow. [claim]

## Cross-book
- The **value** side of the price rule is the Value Equation (concept 04).
- Leads applies a price schedule in warm outreach (LD-022, "price goes up every five"), see concept 13.
- Leads' referral chapter restates price-to-value as "goodwill = value − price" (LD-071), see concept 17.
- Money Models treats pricing as *structure* (payment plans, discounts, trials), see concepts 22–25.

## Do not
- Do not turn the price/value picture into a number; the book gives no formula for it.
- Do not use any case multiple (5x, 38x, 3.1x, 32x) as a forecast.
- Do not tell a client to raise price without also raising delivered/perceived value first (the book's order).
